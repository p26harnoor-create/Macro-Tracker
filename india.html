RBI: first hike in nearly four years, stance to "calibrated tightening" Updated 9 Oct

#

On 7 October the MPC raised the repo rate 25 bp to 5.50% — unanimously — and moved the stance from neutral to calibrated tightening. Inflation forecasts went up, growth forecasts went up too, and the Governor said the next move "can only be a rate hike or a pause."

Fact The hike matched consensus. Governor Malhotra: "The policy action ahead can only be a rate hike or a pause" — near-term cuts are "off the table" — and the extent and timing of further increases "will depend on inflation and growth outcomes." He flagged West Asia re-escalation, crude volatility and a weak monsoon with a strong El Niño as risks to the rabi season and rural demand. Market reaction on the day was modest (10Y 7.2655%, +5 bp; USD/INR ~96.36, flat; Nifty −0.6%, Sensex −0.7%; banks up, autos and realty down). The violent move came the next day, driven by oil and US yields rather than the RBI (see §2.4, §2.7).

View This is the "credibility trade" §2.1 anticipated: hike into a supply shock to stop it generalising, while core ex-precious-metals at 2.9% gives the RBI cover to say it is pre-empting, not chasing. Raising the growth forecast to 7.1% in the same breath is the tell — the RBI believes the economy can absorb tighter policy. The December meeting is now live for a second hike (Goldman: December and February).

Why hike into a supply shock the repo cannot fix:

  • Expectations anchor. If households and firms assume 6–7% inflation, wage demands and pre-emptive price rises make it self-fulfilling. The hike targets the spread, not the source.
  • Currency. A wider India–US differential makes INR assets relatively more attractive and narrows the hedged-yield gap for foreign bond investors (§2.4). On 8 Oct the US 10Y rose further, so the differential barely moved — hence INR through 97.
  • Division of labour. Government fights the source (edible-oil duty cuts, grain releases, export curbs); the RBI stops generalisation.

View "Calibrated tightening" is a signal about the distribution of future moves, not a schedule: it rules out cuts and keeps hikes data-dependent. Transmission will be faster than usual because the liquidity surplus is being drained at the same time.

§2.2

Inflation: a 4.8% headline hiding a 9.9% pipeline

#

The Indian inflation story is not "food prices are high." It is five interacting shocks — food dispersion, oil, a weak rupee, industrial input costs and the risk of second-round pass-through — with an unusually wide gap between consumer and wholesale prices.

4.82%CPI Aug 2026 (Jul 4.45%)
5.95%Food CPI (Jul 5.52%)
5.23 / 4.31Rural / urban CPI
9.92%WPI Aug — the pipeline
+22.9%WPI fuel & power; mineral oils +38.5%
+8.37%WPI manufactured products
2.27%Housing CPI — what is not hot

Fact Within vegetables the dispersion is extreme: tomato −31.1% YoY and potato −13.1%, but onion +48.3%, garlic +43.6%, ginger +73.8%. Silver jewellery +107% and gold jewellery +35.5% are also pushing the index despite small weights (0.31% and 0.62%). On 24 Sept the government cut edible-oil import duties (crude sunflower 10%→0%, soybean and palm 10%→5%) explicitly to curb inflation.

View The number to watch is not 4.82% — it is the ~5-point gap between CPI and WPI. India is between "Stage 1: shock" and "Stage 2: producer inflation." The whole RBI dilemma is whether upstream cost pressure fades before firms pass it into retail prices, wages and services (Stage 3). If yes, this unwinds fast. If no, India moves from a supply-side problem into a much harder second-round one.

1. The inflation map: five shocks

Think of India's inflation as five interacting shocks, of which the first three currently dominate: (1) food supply, (2) energy/oil, (3) weak-rupee imported inflation, (4) industrial input costs, (5) possible second-round pass-through into services and wages. This is not a classic demand-overheating story — which is critical for what the RBI can and cannot fix.

2. Food: dispersion, not "vegetables"

Onions vs tomatoes. Tomatoes are extremely perishable, so small supply changes produce enormous price swings that self-correct: +31.9% YoY in June → −4.6% July → −31.1% August. The RBI should not react to tomatoes. Onions are different: supply is seasonal and depends on stored rabi onions bridging to the next crop, so storage losses → lower mandi arrivals → wholesale ↑ → retail ↑, and the shock persists. Right now tomatoes and potatoes are helping CPI while onion/garlic/ginger are hurting it.

Sugar is a three-way allocation problem: cane → sugar / ethanol / other products. Cane disease (red rot) does not just cut sugar supply — it collides with the ethanol-blending programme competing for the same feedstock. Sugar inflation = crop disease × stocks × ethanol policy × export policy × administered cane prices.

Edible oils may be the bigger near-term food risk. India is heavily import-dependent, so cooking-oil inflation = global price × USD/INR × import duty. With the rupee from ₹85 to ₹96 and global palm/soy/sunflower prices up, India is hit twice. The 24 Sept duty cuts sacrifice tariff revenue to stop the full shock reaching households — the government can cut duties; the RBI cannot produce palm oil.

Cereals are slower and stickier: wheat is vulnerable to terminal heat during grain-filling, and a wheat shock becomes manufactured-food inflation (atta → bread, biscuits, noodles). Rice depends on monsoon, reservoirs, MSP, FCI procurement and export curbs; because the state is such a large participant, stock releases and trade policy move prices. Pulses are a structural protein problem with an FX loop: domestic shortage → imports ↑ → dollar demand ↑ → INR pressure → imported dal costs more. Milk is potentially more persistent than any vegetable because feed costs (maize, oilcake) feed into procurement prices and then into curd, paneer, ghee, sweets and restaurants. And protein demand rising with incomes is a structural shift monetary policy is poor at fixing.

3. Energy: the biggest external shock

August WPI: fuel & power +22.93%, mineral oils +38.48%, crude petroleum & gas +34.41%. CPI is only 4.82% because wholesale energy shocks do not pass one-for-one into retail: firms accept lower margins, government adjusts taxes/duties/subsidies, oil marketing companies smooth pump prices. But absorbers wear out — if the shock persists, pass-through rises.

India experiences the rupee oil price: Brent × USD/INR. $100 × ₹96 = ₹9,600/bbl vs $80 × ₹85 = ₹6,800/bbl — a ~41% difference. Oil enters diesel (tractors, pumps, trucking), aviation, shipping, plastics, packaging, chemicals, fibres, paint, fertilizer. Oil can hurt CPI + WPI + current account + INR + corporate margins + RBI policy simultaneously; onions only hurt CPI.

4. The weak rupee as amplifier

India imports oil, gas, edible oils, fertilizer feedstock, electronics, machinery, chemicals and metals. INR ↓ → landed cost ↑ → producer cost ↑ → WPI ↑ → eventually CPI ↑ (exchange-rate pass-through). The dangerous loop: oil ↑ → import bill ↑ → USD demand ↑ → INR ↓ → oil even dearer in rupees → imported inflation ↑ → RBI more hawkish → conditions tighten.

5. Manufactured goods and the capex tension

WPI manufactured products +8.37%, led by basic metals, chemicals and manufactured food — inflation is spreading through industrial supply chains. There is a mild tension with the private-capex upturn (§2.8): factories, power, transmission, data centres and roads all pull on steel, aluminium, copper and cement; if supply lags, capex demand becomes input inflation. That does not make capex bad; it means the RBI watches for capacity constraints.

6. Supply vs demand inflation, and why the RBI hikes anyway

Supply-driven (oil, bad crop, onions, cane disease, edible-oil imports, weak INR) the RBI cannot directly fix. Demand-driven (credit boom, housing, wages, services above capacity) it can. It hikes into a supply shock because of second-round effects: cost of living ↑ → wage demands ↑ → services prices ↑ → general inflation — and because expectations are the bridge. Not because repo rates produce onions, but to stop onion inflation becoming wage inflation becoming services inflation.

7. Distribution and what is not hot

Rural CPI 5.23% vs urban 4.31%; rural food 6.13% vs urban 5.64%. Food weighs more in rural baskets, so rural purchasing power, FMCG, two-wheelers and farm economics take the hit. Housing at 2.27% is the tell that this is not broad domestic overheating — pressure is concentrated in food + imported commodities + energy + upstream costs. That is a different regime from 2022's.

The three stages

Stage 1 — Shockoil ↑, INR ↓, onions ↑, edible oils ↑, sugar
Stage 2 — Producer inflationfreight, energy, chemicals, metals, food processing → WPI ~10%
Stage 3 — Second roundretail price hikes → wage demands → services → core CPI ↑ → RBI tightens hard

View India is between Stage 1 and 2. The entire outlook hinges on whether it enters Stage 3.

The pipeline, step by step

crude ↑→refinery/input costs→freight→chemicals, plastics, packaging→manufacturer costs→WPI ↑→partial pass-through→CPI ↑

§2.3

Fertilizer, urea & NIPU-2026: a vulnerability turned into a capex story

#

Two stories, not one. Cyclical: Hormuz raised gas, urea and shipping costs while India still imports ~a quarter of its urea — and the subsidy turned that into a fiscal event. Structural: NIPU-2026 is a multi-year attempt to stop being exposed to exactly that shock.

~25%Share of urea requirement imported; world's largest urea importer
15 Jul 2026CCEA approves National Investment Policy for Urea 2026
₹2.04 trn~$23bn of proposals, 17 of them (GAIL, HURL, Kribhco…), 8–9 new plants
12–16%Bounded ROE band replaces open-ended subsidy exposure
4 yearsFixed costs convert to rupees at prevailing FX — the fix for 2012's flaw

Fact NIPU-2026 separates fixed and variable costs, bounds returns at 12–16% ROE and converts fixed costs into rupee terms after four years. It has drawn ~₹2.04 trillion in proposals for 8–9 new plants plus expansions.

View The interesting part is not the ₹2 lakh crore headline. It is that India is trying to convert a recurring macro vulnerability (Middle-East shock → expensive urea → subsidy blowout) into a capex opportunity. The test is execution: proposals ≠ financial closure ≠ construction ≠ commissioned capacity.

Why urea matters so much

India has two layers of external exposure: it imports finished urea and its domestic plants run on imported gas/LNG. So the vulnerability is gas/LNG ↑ + urea ↑ + shipping ↑ + USD/INR ↑ → fertilizer cost ↑ → subsidy bill ↑ → fiscal pressure, and eventually → farm input costs ↑ → food inflation. Hormuz is not just an oil chokepoint — it carries ~20% of global LNG and a large share of fertilizer feedstock trade, so disruption hits crude, gas, urea, shipping insurance and the rupee at once.

The subsidy as shock absorber — and where the shock goes

Farmers pay a controlled price; the state absorbs the gap. If imported urea goes ₹25,000 → ₹35,000/tonne, the government chooses where the shock appears: let farm costs rise (food inflation) or hold the farmer price (fiscal expenditure). The shock has not disappeared — it moved from the farmer's balance sheet to the government's. That is why "Hormuz became a fiscal event" is exactly right, and why the first-round CPI impact is muted.

The fiscal loop then runs: subsidy ↑ → deficit pressure → borrowing ↑ → G-Sec supply ↑ → yields ↑ — the opposite of the fiscal-consolidation → less crowding-out → private capex ↑ story in §2.6. Hormuz is connected to Indian G-Secs.

Currency multiplies it

Urea $400/t at ₹85 = ₹34,000/t. Urea $500/t at ₹96 = ₹48,000/t — a 41% increase in rupee terms, the same arithmetic as crude. India's fertilizer vulnerability is three vulnerabilities at once: commodity, chokepoint and currency.

What NIPU-2026 changes

  • Bounded 12–16% ROE narrows the distribution of outcomes; bankability improves because uncertainty falls, not because the ceiling is spectacular.
  • Fixed/variable separation stops investors unknowingly taking gas-price risk; banks can lend against clearer economics.
  • FX clause: fixed costs convert to rupees after four years at the prevailing rate, so a move from ₹85 to ₹100 no longer wrecks project returns — the state absorbs part of the long-run FX uncertainty that killed the 2012 policy.

Caveats that make the answer better

  • Domestic urea still needs gas. Ammonia needs hydrogen, which today comes from natural gas. New plants cut finished-urea imports, not energy dependence — unless paired with domestic gas, green hydrogen/ammonia and fertilizer-use efficiency.
  • Overbuild risk. If demand undershoots or global urea collapses, plants need continued support; the ROE framework is what keeps capacity from becoming permanently uneconomic.
  • Nutrient balance. Over-subsidised urea distorts N:P:K use. Real fertilizer security = more capacity + better balance + higher efficiency.
  • Proposal ≠ capex. Proposal → approval → financing → land/environment clearance → EPC award → construction → commissioning → utilisation. Track what reaches financial closure.

Spillovers

Fertilizer plants pull EPC, steel, cement, compressors, pumps, pipelines, power and engineering services — so fertilizers/chemicals join power, data centres and metals as a private-capex theme (§2.8). Substituting $5–10bn/yr of imports structurally lowers USD demand and INR vulnerability — the same logic as oil, but far more achievable than crude independence.

§2.4

USD/INR & the dollar: it is the US long end, not DXY Updated 9 Oct

#

The rupee broke 97 on 8 October, the day after the RBI hiked. Four forces — 5.35% Treasuries, euro-led dollar strength, $105 oil and FPI exits — are reinforcing each other, while the RBI smooths the path rather than defending a level.

5.35%US 10Y intraday 8 Oct — highest since 2002
~190 bpIndia 10Y (7.27%) minus US 10Y — gone after a 2% INR fall
₹31,282 crFPI selling 1–8 Oct (incl. primary); Sept ₹35,861 cr

What changed, 7–9 OctThe RBI's 25 bp hike held the rupee flat for one day (96.36 on 7 Oct). On 8 Oct Brent jumped 5% to $105.30 on reports of US–Iran escalation and Houthi strikes on Saudi airports, the US 10Y hit 5.35% after Fed minutes and Waller pointed to further hikes, and FPIs kept selling — USD/INR closed at 97.13. Every leg of the "dangerous scenario" in the Watch tab fired at once. View A 25 bp domestic hike cannot offset a simultaneous move in oil and the US long end; it can only keep the adjustment orderly, which it did.

Fact Reuters had attributed early-October rupee pressure partly to "euro-led dollar strength," high US yields and expectations of continued RBI smoothing. The 6 Oct relief (oil −3%, 10Y 5.27%) lasted two sessions.

View The most important variable at the start of the chain is not DXY but the US 10Y/30Y. If US long yields sustainably retreat while oil keeps falling, a large part of India's external pressure unwinds before the RBI does anything dramatic.

A. US yields are the hurdle rate

A global fund can earn ~5.3% for ten years in dollars with no INR risk, no credit risk and deep liquidity. An Indian asset must compensate for equity + INR + oil + macro risk on top. That raises the hurdle for all EM assets. And this is happening while Fed hike expectations ease — because long yields reflect US fiscal concerns, Treasury supply, term premium and inflation uncertainty, not just the next FOMC (see US §3.2). A Fed pause alone may not save EMs if the 10Y stays ~5%.

B. DXY is not "the dollar vs the world"

DXY is dominated by the euro, then yen, sterling, CAD, SEK, CHF. It can rise because EUR falls with nothing happening in India — yet USD/INR dealers see DXY ↑, US yields ↑, Asian FX ↓ and sell rupees. Part of the move is global USD strength, not India-specific weakness. But India has its own problems too.

C. FPI selling creates dollar demand

Follow the transaction: a US fund sells ₹1,000 cr of Nifty stocks, receives rupees, wants dollars → sells INR, buys USD, repatriates. Equity selling is FX selling. The loop: equity −5% and INR −5% = −10% in dollars → India looks worse → more selling. This is the loop the RBI wants to stop becoming disorderly.

D. Oil companies are the second dollar buyer

IOC/BPCL/HPCL need dollars for crude. Brent ↑ → import bill ↑ → USD demand ↑ → INR ↓. The FX market therefore has three big dollar buyers at once: oil companies, other importers, FPIs exiting. Any one alone is manageable; all together they reinforce.

E. How RBI intervention works — and its side effects

RBI sells USD, receives INR → dollar scarcity eases → ₹96→97→98 becomes orderly rather than violent. But selling dollars removes rupee liquidity — a tightening operation — at the same time as FCNR(B) inflows add liquidity and OMO sales remove it (§2.5). The RBI is juggling FX stability, banking liquidity, inflation and bond yields simultaneously; the repo rate alone misses most of what it is doing. And the RBI does not want ₹96 forever: it manages the path, not the destination. Permanently fighting global fundamentals burns reserves.

F. India's FX equation

USD demandUSD supply
Oil, gold, electronics, machinery and other imports; external debt service; FPI exitsIT/services exports, goods exports, remittances, FDI, FPI inflows, external borrowing, FCNR(B), RBI reserves

India is unusual: a large goods deficit offset by a huge services surplus and remittances. Right now the demand side is unusually powerful.

G. Where it bites: bonds, equities, gold

  • Bonds. India 10Y 7.2% vs US 5.3% = ~190 bp. A 5% INR fall erases it; nominal spread ≠ foreign-investor return. Hedged, the comparison is Indian yield − hedge cost vs Treasuries — another reason high US yields cut foreign demand for G-Secs.
  • Equities. Two hits: relative attractiveness (why take Indian equity risk at 5.3% risk-free → P/E compresses) and currency (Nifty +8% with INR −8% = zero in USD). DIIs do not have the FX problem — the SIP machine is India's shock absorber (§2.7).
  • Gold. Normally USD ↑ and real yields ↑ are bad for gold; yet it is very elevated — a signal that geopolitical, fiscal and central-bank demand is overriding the macro. For Indian buyers, price = international gold × USD/INR + duties, so rupee weakness lifts domestic gold even when USD gold is flat.

H. Imported inflation arithmetic

₹90 → ₹96 is ~6.7% depreciation; a $100 import goes from ₹9,000 to ₹9,600. Pass-through is lagged, but with WPI already ~10% the depreciation is more dangerous than it would be in a low-commodity environment. That is why RBI easing probability falls and hike probability rises — and why the dollar indirectly sets Indian yields.

The full chain, America → India

Oil ↑→refiners need more USD→INR ↓ further+Indian equities weak→FPIs sell, convert INR→USD→INR ↓ further

FCNR(B) & liquidity: $127bn in, and the RBI is draining it Added

#

The 2026 FCNR(B) window raised almost five times the 2013 taper-tantrum amount. It bought rupee stability and record reserves — and left the banking system with a record ₹11.6 lakh crore surplus the RBI is now absorbing while inflation rises.

$127.2bnFCNR(B) raised by 31 Aug 2026 ($136.4bn incl. ECBs/overseas borrowing)
4.9×vs 2013's ~$26bn
₹11.6 lakh crRecord system liquidity surplus, 7 Sept (~4% of deposits)
₹7 lakh cr30-day VRRR announced 4 Sept; ₹2.59 lakh cr accepted on 7 Sept
$740.8bnRecord FX reserves, 28 Aug

Fact The scheme opened 8 June 2026 for fresh 3–5 year deposits, with the RBI taking on the hedging cost (estimated ~3–3.5%), CRR/SLR exemptions and leveraged structures permitted. The deposit deadline was pulled forward to 31 Aug (swaps by 11 Sept). Over ₹8.5 lakh crore was absorbed through overnight and term VRRR in early September.

View This is the hidden hand behind the bond and rupee moves. The RBI is simultaneously adding dollars (reserves, intervention capacity) and subtracting rupees (VRRR, OMO sales, FX sales). It explains why short rates and the 364-day bill have risen before any repo move — the liquidity regime has already tightened.

What the scheme did

A bank raises a 3–5 year dollar deposit from an NRI, swaps the dollars with the RBI at a concessional rate (RBI bears the hedging cost), and is exempt from CRR/SLR on it. The bank gets cheap rupee funding; the RBI gets dollars onto its balance sheet; the NRI gets an attractive dollar return. It is the 2013 playbook, scaled: ~$26bn then, $127bn now.

Why it created a liquidity problem

Each swap hands banks rupees. Surplus rose from ₹9.7 lakh crore (3 Sept) to ₹11.6 lakh crore (7 Sept). Unabsorbed, that pushes overnight rates toward the floor and loosens conditions exactly when inflation is rising. So the RBI announced a ₹7 lakh crore 30-day VRRR with early redemption, ran overnight operations of ₹3.5 lakh crore, and sold G-Secs via OMO — withdrawing more than ₹6 lakh crore in one day.

The three-way juggling act

RBI sells USD to smooth INR↓ (drains)Reserves ↓, INR supportneutral/↑

View OMO sales are the piece bond investors resent most: the RBI is adding duration supply to a market already absorbing ~₹8 trillion of H2 issuance (§2.6). That is a big part of why the 10Y is 7.21% and the 5Y 7.08% with the repo unchanged.

The 2013 lesson: FCNR(B) stabilises the currency quickly but shifts the problem onto the liquidity and bond-supply side. In 2026 the shift is five times larger.

View The long end is being hit from three sides — expected policy rates, duration supply (government + RBI OMO sales), and a thinner foreign bid as the hedged spread to Treasuries compresses. None of these is about India's fiscal credibility per se; the consolidation path is intact. That is why the 7.4–7.5% calls are a pressure range, not a crisis call.

Decomposing the move

  • Policy expectations. The 364-day bill +24 bp and 1Y OIS +21 bp say the market moved from "cuts are over" to "two hikes." That alone lifts the whole curve.
  • Supply. ~₹8 trillion of H2 issuance plus incremental OMO sales (§2.5). Primary dealers are capacity-constrained, so auction cut-offs tail.
  • Foreign bid. With US 10Y at 5.3%, the unhedged India–US spread is ~190 bp and the hedged spread is thin; FPI debt demand softens (§2.4).
  • Inflation premium. WPI ~10% raises the odds that RBI's 5.9% Q3 CPI forecast is exceeded.

Crowding out, and the fertilizer wrinkle

The medium-term story investors were trading: fiscal consolidation → lower borrowing → less G-Sec supply → lower yields → lower corporate cost of capital → less crowding out → more private capex. The 2026 fertilizer subsidy shock (§2.3) is a counter-current: subsidy ↑ → expenditure ↑ → borrowing pressure ↑. Whether the government absorbs it within the deficit target (via buoyant tax receipts, RBI dividend, expenditure switching) or lets borrowing rise is a key H2 question.

Who holds the duration

Banks (SLR + HTM), insurers and pension/provident funds are structural buyers; mutual funds and FPIs are the marginal, price-sensitive bid. When the marginal bid steps back and the RBI is a net seller, the price adjustment falls on auctions — hence the tails.

Fact On the August policy day, by contrast, FIIs bought ₹2,446 crore as the rupee rallied to 94.89 — flows follow the currency.

View Higher US yields hit Indian equities twice: relative attractiveness (P/E compression) and currency (FPIs measure in dollars). DIIs measure in rupees and do not have the FX problem — which is why FPI sells / DII buys is the defining pattern of this cycle.

The FPI calculus

The foreign investor's question is "why take Indian equity risk when Treasuries yield 5.3%?" — the required equity return rises, so the multiple India can sustain falls. Then the currency: even a good year in rupees can be zero in dollars. Equity selling is itself FX selling (§2.4), so the loop equity ↓ → INR ↓ → USD returns ↓ → more selling is live.

The DII counterweight

Indian mutual funds, insurers and pension funds measure wealth in rupees. Monthly SIP inflows are sticky, price-insensitive and growing, giving the market a structural bid that did not exist at this scale in 2013 or 2018. View Without it, the equity-market impact of today's FPI selling could plausibly be much larger — a valuation cushion, not a valuation argument.
